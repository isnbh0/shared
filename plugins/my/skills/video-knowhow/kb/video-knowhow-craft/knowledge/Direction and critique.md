# Direction and whole piece critique

Before assembling scenes, define the audience, the premise, and the change in understanding or feeling you want the piece to cause. A useful direction gives each scene a job and gives the ending something specific to answer. This planning method is an editorial one, drawn from [visual identity](../../video-knowhow-code/theory/Visual%20identity%20and%20style.md), the C comparison summarized in [the comparison runs](../cases/Comparison%20runs.md), and the [SLASH_MY case](../cases/SLASH_MY.md).

## Decide what varies, what stays consistent, and what recurs

**Variation** can come from scale, framing, mechanism, palette, camera, typography, or visual rhythm. If all of them vary with no shared purpose, the piece can fall apart into fragments. If all of them repeat, different claims can look interchangeable.

**Consistency** can sit in the emotional line, the integrity of the information, legibility, the level of execution, or the relation between an action and its consequence. Scenes do not have to look alike to be consistent. SLASH_MY's creative aim was varied visuals, themes, and rhythm with consistent pathos and quality. Its documented response includes a distinct visual setting and mechanism for each section, connected by an arc from frustration to competence to elation.

**Recurrence** should develop a meaning. SLASH_MY puts that aim into practice with three motifs: the caret stands for John, typing John's own real complaints, the strike for the fix, and the musical hook for the film's musical identity. A different film could use a moving measurement, a recurring question, or a camera position it returns to. Use [motif development](../patterns/Motif%20development.md) to decide what each return adds.

## Turn a concept into scenes

Write the premise in one sentence that names the subject and the consequence. Then outline the states the audience needs to see. For each scene, name:

1. The new information or emotional turn.
2. The main visible relationship and the state in which it is complete and readable.
3. The action that reveals or changes that relationship.
4. What continues from the previous scene and what the next scene picks up.
5. The musical phrase and which sound is in the foreground.
6. The inspection that would show the scene failing.

A visual metaphor needs a mapping: what each important part stands for, and where the metaphor stops being literal. In SLASH_MY, a page becomes a node in a graph, which stands for a document becoming findable within a larger set. The [page path](../cases/Page%20to%20larger%20context.md) keeps that same page on screen. A decorative zoom into an unrelated graph would weaken the relationship.

Use a reference to find a relationship you can control: a readable title scale, contrast between sections, attack and release, or a convincing handoff. Write down how its brief and audience differ from yours. D-001 came out derivative but acceptable, and D-002 made a weaker new picture. They show why borrowing a source that works, and copying a list of its treatments, does not prove that its execution comes along.

## Setup, payoff, and restraint

The AX DAY reconstruction names the event in its opening request and holds back the result. SLASH_MY holds back the complete identity and brings back the earlier complaints as habits that have been answered. Both tie the ending to an expectation set up earlier, by different mechanisms. See [meaningful reveal](../patterns/Meaningful%20reveal.md).

Quiet helps when the viewer needs to read, anticipate, feel confident, or recover. For each proposed detail, ask what it adds and how you will judge whether it added that. SLASH_MY's writing valley is one answer to the dense sequence around it, and other films may answer density without a paper section. [Density and rest](../patterns/Density%20and%20rest.md) gives other options.

## Critique the whole piece

First watch for the experience you intended: what did the opening promise, what changed, and did the ending answer it? Then inspect scene variety, transitions, typography, material, motion, music, and sound one at a time to find where things fail. After a revision, replay the whole piece, because a stronger impact in one place can distort the larger arc. Across rounds, [convergence](Convergence.md) covers the closing decisions: what a review may reopen, which judgments stay frozen, and when to stop.

A contact sheet shows repeated framing and scale, but it cannot show suspense or groove. A technical audit can find clipping, resets, and missing state; evidence about whether the audience understood and was convinced has to come from the audience. John reported that SLASH_MY was the most polished and convincing film, a verdict on the [whole film](../cases/SLASH_MY.md#what-this-case-is).

**Evaluate**: in a comparison copy, remove one decorative layer or one redundant scene. Does the premise get weaker, or easier to follow? Ask a viewer to describe the ending's consequence without your explanation.
