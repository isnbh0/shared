# Attention, time, and meaning

Decide what the viewer must notice, read, and infer, and then decide which relationship motion should make visible. The [theory map](../../video-knowhow-code/maps/Theory.md) holds the established reference; this page connects it to production decisions.

## Give attention a destination

[Hierarchy](../../video-knowhow-code/theory/Attention%20and%20hierarchy.md) works by relation: a large title can lose attention to a smaller counter that flashes. Finish the still frame so it informs on its own, then pick one primary change for each story beat. Supporting movement can follow that change without giving the viewer a second thing to read. In a [typographic event](Typographic%20events.md), this is why the reveal, subtitle, sheen, and credit need separate roles and separate timing.

Proximity and alignment form groups, and movement can keep or break them. [Grouping and continuity](../../video-knowhow-code/theory/Grouping%20and%20continuity.md) explains why a label must travel with the object it describes. A shared color alone is weak evidence that two things are the same when many objects share that color. The [page pullback](../cases/Page%20to%20larger%20context.md) makes the relationship concrete by keeping the document itself on screen.

## Spend time according to the reading task

[Reading time](../../video-knowhow-code/theory/Moving-text%20legibility.md) begins once the essential text is complete and stable enough to read. Entrance duration, settling, and reading time are separate budgets. If the last glyph arrives just before a cut, a shot that is long on paper may leave little reading time. For data, keep the units and the reference baseline on screen during movement, and keep the endpoints afterward.

[Temporal rhythm](../../video-knowhow-code/theory/Temporal%20visual%20rhythm.md) includes anticipation, event, hold, and recovery. Rest can build confidence or suspense. Repeating an accent without changing its role can flatten the emphasis. Before adding secondary motion, compare the same passage with a longer completed state. [Density and rest](../patterns/Density%20and%20rest.md) connects this local choice to the sequence and the score.

## Preserve the claim

A display transition is not a measured intermediate value. Keep marks, labels, and units consistent with the quantity they represent. An animated bar that briefly stretches past its value states a false number, so put emphasis in a separate outline or annotation and leave the mark's geometry alone. Say whether an intermediate frame is an observation, a simulation, or a display transition: a smooth move between two measured endpoints creates display values, and those values make the transition's claim. Keep labels attached when sorting or regrouping. As a rule of thumb, a repair animation illustrates how the fix works; a claim about a real product's speed or correctness comes from a recording of that product.

Perceived weight comes from the relation between path, motion spacing, contact, and response; no single spring parameter sets it. See [motion character](Motion%20character.md). Color, contrast, type roles, and spacing stay relational decisions from static design. Prominence comes from several cues acting together in a composition. When a hierarchy feels wrong, the default diagnosis compares the elements together at their intended scale, and a single property checked alone can miss how the cues combine. Give each one visual force in proportion to its role, its urgency, and its importance to the task.

## Make meaning available under different conditions

Use [accessibility and perception](../../video-knowhow-code/knowledge/Accessibility%20and%20perception.md). When large movement must be reduced, the calmer version keeps the same required information: completed labels and values shown at once, static steps, or a sequence the reader controls. A browser's motion preference changes only browser animation, so motion already encoded in an MP4 or GIF needs its own calmer or static alternative when one is required. Muted viewing should still get the core claim across, and captions should stay stable while emphasis moves.

**Evaluate**: at the intended display size, find the first focal event, the first complete claim, and the first competing event. Then ask an intended viewer what changed. If they cannot name the result, check the wording and hierarchy before making the motion more complex.
