# Focused comparison

Use a focused comparison when the viewer needs to spot a difference that matters. Hold the reference conditions steady, change the property your claim is about, and then keep both endpoints, or enough shared context, on screen to compare them accurately.

## Construct a fair view

Name the question first: amount, position, correctness, speed, or appearance. Keep labels, units, scale, and identity consistent with that question. In a chart, changing the axis while the values change gives the viewer two possible causes for any movement they see. In a material study, changing silhouette, lighting, and crop at once cannot isolate the effect of grain.

Choose the display that fits the question. A split divider moving across one subject compares exact endpoints frame by frame. An overlay shows whether two states line up. A sequential change follows one object's identity through the transition. Keep geometry true to the quantities shown, so that an animated bar never stretches past its value, and stage data changes so each step shows only the change that carries the meaning. Keep the label still if the surface or camera moves.

Sound can mark the boundary of a comparison, but louder audio can bias a viewer toward the picture it plays with. When you judge sound, or picture and sound together, match playback level where appropriate and record which streams changed. When you explain data, a dramatic impact must not suggest a magnitude the geometry does not support.

## Learn from bounded examples

[D-003](../knowledge/Form%20material%20and%20depth.md) compares matched stills at 10 and 18 seconds. Its variants separate join repair, grain placement, and inner-depth treatment: J repairs the contour joins, G keeps J's form and moves the grain behind it, and D keeps G's front outline and adds a clipped dark aperture that exposes an inner wall. The record also documents the confounds that remain. J changes more than one drawing defect at once, so its benefit cannot be assigned to one join, and the plain captures leave out parts of the compositor. John tentatively preferred J in both rows, then questioned the form itself.

[D-007](../cases/Comparison%20runs.md) changes an arrival envelope and holds the rest of the picture and sound fixed. The immediate and delayed arrivals differed in their exact frames on the beat, but John found the difference hard to tell and not meaningful at playback speed. Its one arrival parameter changes opacity and displacement together, and the run does not isolate either.

These runs show ways to structure an inspection. A comparison that explains something to the public may need only a simpler paired display, while a diagnostic test may need matched variants and blind labels, as in the C2 one-kick ablation, where John rated both blind excerpts bad. Neither kind should hide a condition that bears on its conclusion.

**Evaluate**: can a viewer name the difference without being told where to look? Does the presentation support only the intended claim? For a preference test, allow “not sure,” keep negative findings, and replay enough context to judge the passage's role in the sequence.
