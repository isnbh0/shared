# Continuity carriers

Before you choose a transition, name what should survive it: an object, a relation, a camera direction, a shape, a phrase, or a sound. That carrier helps the viewer understand why the next scene follows. A smooth transition between two unrelated things can imply a relationship that does not exist.

## Construct the handoff

Write down the outgoing state, the incoming state, and what they share. For a visible object, include position, scale, orientation, crop, color role, and which scene draws it on each side. For motion, include direction and the velocity at the boundary. For sound, include the position in the phrase and any tail or incoming cue that crosses the cut. The [workbench](../knowledge/Production%20workbench.md) turns this record into an implementation contract.

The [SLASH_MY page pullback](../cases/Page%20to%20larger%20context.md) keeps the page the viewer has just read and turns it into a node in a document graph. The pull reaches the boundary still moving and then cuts to rest: one boundary combines a camera move with an intentional cut. The source snapshot specifies the page geometry, the freeze time, the camera mapping, and the draw ownership, and keeping the page itself makes the change of context understandable. The case page also gives the recorded revision that moved the boundary's second sync point from 250 to 100 ms early.

## Alternatives and tradeoffs

Every transition states a relationship between the scenes. A cut marks a new subject, a matched element connects two representations of one thing, and the viewer takes matched forms as one continuing thing; a matched shape is one way to make that connection. When the scenes share no identity, a clean labeled cut is the carrier. A shared sound phrase can carry continuity across a strong picture cut, and an incoming sound can prepare the new scene before the picture arrives, as the incoming clave does under the outgoing page in the pullback. Within a frame, an object keeps its identity across states by keeping its key and label while its position or role changes.

Each carrier has a cost. A dissolve gives overlap but can mix two sets of words on screen for a moment; treat that overlap as part of the transition, and do not expect precise reading during it. A camera move can keep the space continuous but lose readable scale, and a fixed overview with a labeled inset can keep context better than moving the whole scene. The page pullback's case makes the same point for a dense factual diagram, where an inset may keep comparison better than shrinking the page. A sound tail can bridge two scenes and also mask the next cue. Keep the relationship you need and check what it costs. The two standard carrier mechanisms are the match cut (matched shapes at the same position and size) and the crossfade (a simultaneous fade-out and fade-in). Camera moves form their own family (push-in, pan, and parallax), and each reads differently.

**Evaluate**: inspect the frames just before, on, and after the seam. Trace one named element and listen across the same interval. Is the carrier duplicated, reset, or replaced? For the page pullback, that means the last frame before the cut, the cut, and the first frames of the graph unfolding: is the page drawn once, and can a viewer find it in the graph at normal speed? Then watch the surrounding scenes at speed and listen for whether the seam interrupts the phrase.
