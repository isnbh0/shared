# Density and rest

Vary how much new information arrives over time and how many events compete at once. Rest gives the sequence room for reading, anticipation, or recovery. A rest can still contain quiet, continuous movement, and a passage with few cuts can still be dense when what it says keeps changing.

## Compose contrast with a purpose

For one passage, mark the main visual event, the supporting changes, the words to read, the musical foreground, and the major sound cues on the same timeline. Look for collisions nobody planned and for long stretches that ask the viewer to do the same thing over and over. Give each story beat one primary change: one focus, one main action, and one or two supporting actions. Smaller movements should reinforce that change. [Temporal rhythm](../../video-knowhow-code/theory/Temporal%20visual%20rhythm.md) explains the wider relation between events and holds.

In [SLASH_MY](../cases/SLASH_MY.md), the writing section, with its paper palette, is a calmer valley in picture and music between denser material. At this snapshot, the brief places it at beats 76–96, just before navigation at 96–104, and the section changes ground, camera behavior, and groove together. Its page then becomes the document in the navigation graph, so the rest hands an object to the next rise. Palette, camera, reading, groove, and the section's role in the sequence work together here, and that combination belongs to SLASH_MY. Inserting a pale scene would copy only one of those ingredients.

In [the AX DAY reconstruction](../cases/Anticipation%20to%20title.md), slow clauses give way to faster terms and a countdown. A short gap before the drop interrupts that accelerating build, and a longer hold on the title gives the event information time. The two rests do different jobs and should not share one duration.

## Adjust the right dimension

If reading is hard, show less at once or keep one region of the frame stable. If the sequence lacks contrast, try reducing motion, sound layers, harmonic activity, or visual detail, and compare the results; a cut to black is not the only way to get contrast. If a quiet passage starts to drift, strengthen its question or its held image before adding texture. An atmospheric film may hold one low-density state on purpose for much longer than a promo would.

**Evaluate**: watch and listen across the whole rise, rest, and return. Does the quieter passage still give the viewer something to follow? Does the later peak feel earned, or only louder? A contact sheet can show visual contrast, but it cannot judge how long the viewer stays in each state. Compare alternate timings in continuous playback, and control loudness when you compare sound treatments.
