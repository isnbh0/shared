Motion production · A numbered field guide

Motion production: a Tractatus roundup

Global glossary
TermMeaning here
PieceThe complete film, animation, or interactive presentation being made.
SceneA stretch of the piece that presents one situation or change, with its own visual and sound purpose. In SLASH_MY's visual-grounding scene, a layout reflows wrong and is then repaired; a scene can have a clear boundary even when some elements continue across it.
SequenceA run of connected scenes.
ViewerA member of the audience: the person who has to read, understand, or feel the result.
ScopeHow much of the piece a choice governs, from one component up to the whole direction. Scope is a separate attribute from difficulty and from duration.
TechniqueA way of producing an effect in a particular representation. A mask reveal and stepped motion are techniques.
PatternA reusable approach to a problem that recurs in scenes, sequences, or direction. Defect to repair is one: show a defect, then the action that repairs it, then the fixed result.
RecipeOne concrete, timed arrangement of several operations to adapt, paired with an example you can inspect. Its numbers fit its own content.
CaseAn account of one production, a pair of productions, or a series of runs or tests: what it set out to do, what it produced, what was observed, and the limits of the account. Each case says how it is known, whether from a recorded source version, the chatlog, or run notes.

Each section and subsection opens with a glossary that names its terms and links each one to its definition in the package Glossary. A local glossary adds to the global glossary and to its parent section’s glossary, and a link to another glossary brings in that glossary’s terms. The numbered leaves make claims, give examples, or propose checks; they do not open further subsections.

This roundup is drawn from Video know-how: craft, and its claims about cases keep that shelf’s source snapshot and review limits. Each link leads to the fuller explanation.

  1. 1 Give the piece a purpose that each scene can serve.

    1. 1.0 · Section glossary
      TermMeaning here
      PremiseThe subject the piece presents and the consequence it shows.
      DirectionThe choices that govern a piece's audience, premise, visual style, and how its emotion progresses from start to end.
      SetupInformation that creates an expectation. A build that points toward a title that has not appeared yet is a setup.
      PayoffA result that answers or changes the expectation a setup created, as when the awaited title finally arrives.

    2. 1.1 State what the viewer should understand or feel by the end.

      1. 1.1.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the global glossary and direction glossary.
        Scene jobThe new information or emotional change a scene adds to the piece.

      2. 1.1.1 Write the premise in one sentence that names the subject and what happens to it. Then give each scene a job that supports that sentence.

      3. 1.1.2 For each scene, identify the complete image the viewer must read, the action that changes it, what arrives from the previous scene, and what continues into the next.

      4. 1.1.3 When a page becomes a node in a graph, the move can show that the document belongs to a larger collection. Keep the page and make clear what the graph represents. The viewer needs that mapping to follow the camera move.

      5. 1.1.4 Ask a first-time viewer to describe what the ending changed, and compare their account with the premise. Source: direction and critique.

    3. 1.2 Vary the scenes while keeping their purpose clear.

      1. 1.2.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the direction glossary.
        VariationChange across scenes in framing, scale, material, mechanism, or rhythm.
        ConsistencyThe relationships that stay dependable while other things vary, for example one palette and one type family across every scene.
        MotifA recognizable element that recurs and whose role can develop. SLASH_MY's strike and its hook are motifs.

      2. 1.2.1 Scenes can differ in composition and action. In each one, keep the claims accurate, the required text readable, and the consequences understandable.

      3. 1.2.2 SLASH_MY’s documented direction moves from frustration through competence to elation, and its execution, design, writing, and navigation passages each use a different visual world. That is one way to answer its brief.

      4. 1.2.3 The AX DAY reconstruction names the event in the opening request and holds back the finished video. SLASH_MY holds back its complete identity. Decide what to reveal from the question the opening raises.

      5. 1.2.4 To review a decorative layer or a redundant scene, remove it from a comparison copy and check whether the premise becomes harder or easier to follow. Sources: direction, the SLASH_MY case.

  2. 2 Give viewers enough time to read the title, labels, and result.

    1. 2.0 · Section glossary
      TermMeaning here
      Visual hierarchyThe likely order in which a viewer notices and reads the elements in a frame. Size, contrast, position, isolation, and movement can change that order.
      Story beatA moment that changes what the viewer understands or expects: an explanatory or dramatic moment that matters to the story.
      SettlingBecoming stable enough for its task after any leftover motion. A title that overshoots and wobbles has settled once it is still enough to read.
      Reading timeThe interval when the complete text is stable and readable, before the next competing event. It can be much shorter than the time the text is on screen: a line whose last word arrives just before the cut gets little reading time.

    2. 2.1 Give each story beat one primary change.

      1. 2.1.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the attention glossary.
        Primary changeThe action the viewer should follow first in a story beat.
        Supporting movementMovement that helps the primary change without giving the viewer another thing to track, like an underline that reinforces the word it marks.
        GroupingThe visual relationships that make elements read as belonging together: nearness, shared color, or common motion.

      2. 2.1.1 Compose a still that reads well on its own before planning how it enters, and decide what the viewer should notice first. A small flashing counter can pull attention away from a large title.

      3. 2.1.2 Move a label with the object it describes. Keep the two visibly associated when objects sort, shrink, or change groups.

      4. 2.1.3 A title reveal, a supporting line, a credit, and a highlight sweep each need a different role. Schedule them so the viewer can follow those roles in order. Source: foundations.

    3. 2.2 Count reading time from the last required arrival.

      1. 2.2.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the attention glossary.
        EntranceMovement that brings content into view, like a word sliding or fading in.
        StaggerAn offset between the start times of repeated animations, so each letter of a word, say, starts its entrance a little after the one before.
        Complete stateThe point when every element a task needs is in place. For a line of text, that is when its last word has arrived and stopped moving.

      2. 2.2.1 Entrance, settling, and reading time each take their own share of the shot’s time. A long shot can hold little reading time if its last word arrives just before the cut.

      3. 2.2.2 For equal animation durations and stagger intervals, last completion equals start + duration + (element count − 1) × interval. Recalculate it when the wording changes.

      4. 2.2.3 At delivery size, review the first complete phrase and the next event that competes with it. If the interval between them is too short, shorten the copy, reduce the stagger, or delay the next event.

      5. 2.2.4 Keep the title, essential labels, and final result visible with the sound muted or motion reduced. Where a calmer or static version is required, an encoded video needs a separate version. A browser preference cannot change its frames. Sources: foundations, motion character.

    4. 2.3 Keep chart values and product behavior accurate.

      1. 2.3.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the global glossary.
        Observed valueA value that was actually recorded or measured. An interpolated value is filled in between observed ones.
        InterpolationA value computed between two known values. When a chart's bar grows from one recorded figure to the next, its heights in between are interpolated.
        Reference baselineThe reference from which a quantity is shown, like the line a chart's bars rise from.
        SimulationBehavior built to illustrate a process: particles driven by simulated physics, or a staged product demo.

      2. 2.3.1 In data animation, keep the units, reference baselines, labels, and object identity, and hold the endpoints long enough to compare them.

      3. 2.3.2 Label generated in-between values when viewers could mistake them for observations. An eased progress display can imply that a process changed speed.

      4. 2.3.3 A repair animation can explain how something works. A claim about the product needs evidence of how the product actually behaves, and any simulation or retiming has to be made clear. Sources: foundations, make a deliverable.

  3. 3 Build each component so its movement, form, text, and surface support the same event.

    1. 3.0 · Section glossary
      TermMeaning here
      ComponentA text group, object, or other part of a scene: a title, a button, a chart.
      PathThe route an object follows through the frame.
      Motion spacingAn object's positions at equal time intervals along its path: positions bunched together read as slow movement, positions spread apart as fast.
      FormThe silhouette, proportions, and contours of an object.
      MaterialThe surface appearance of a form: matte paper, glossy plastic, brushed metal.
      DepthThe visual relationships that make space and recession visible. One object hiding another, or an inner wall showing through an opening, both show depth.

    2. 3.1 Choose the movement’s meaning before tuning its curve.

      1. 3.1.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the component glossary and settling definition.
        EasingHow progress changes over time along a movement. Ease-in starts slowly and speeds up; ease-out slows into a stop.
        RecoilA response after contact or arrival, like a small bounce back when an object lands.
        Follow-throughMovement that continues after the main body stops, like a ribbon that keeps swinging.
        Stepped timeTime held at discrete intervals for one layer, so that layer moves in visible steps. A 12 fps clock inside 60 fps output is stepped time.

      2. 3.1.1 Decide whether the movement should suggest arrival, force, flexibility, procedure, or instability. Then coordinate path, motion spacing, contact, and response to match.

      3. 3.1.2 Changing the easing changes progress along a path. Compare positions at equal time steps to see acceleration, check the largest excursion for cropping, and check the tail for whether it holds still enough to read.

      4. 3.1.3 At the cited SLASH_MY snapshot, a 12 fps pain clock inside 60 fps output holds each state for five frames. All shutter samples see that held state, so the instability stays sharp.

      5. 3.1.4 Keep that clock local to the defect. The repair can then use continuous movement while the rest of the piece stays smooth. Sources: motion character, jitter to repair.

    3. 3.2 Treat the title as an event with a readable result.

      1. 3.2.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the component glossary and reading time definition.
        GlyphA single drawn letter or symbol, as a font renders it.
        Target geometryThe positions and shapes an entrance resolves into. In the AX DAY reconstruction, particles gather into the letterforms of the title.
        CompositingCombining separately drawn layers into one image, using each layer's alpha to decide what shows through.
        HeadroomThe margin left before a signal reaches its ceiling: for audio, how far the level sits below full scale; for a lit surface, how much brightness is left before display white.

      2. 3.2.1 Measure the actual font and wording before placing particle targets, masks, underlines, and supporting copy, and recompute those positions when the text changes.

      3. 3.2.2 In the cited AX DAY reconstruction, the impact begins at 16 seconds and solid type is fully opaque at 17.1 seconds, while supporting information keeps arriving after that. Each line’s reading time starts at its own complete state.

      4. 3.2.3 Layer order and effect duration affect readability. The source draws solid type over the particles, and its strongest glitch mostly ends before the viewer has to read the letters.

      5. 3.2.4 A moving sheen needs brightness headroom on the letter faces. Check whether it travels across the surface or only enlarges a halo. Source: typographic events.

    4. 3.3 Inspect form before adding surface detail.

      1. 3.3.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the component glossary.
        OcclusionOne surface hiding another that sits behind it.
        ApertureAn opening through which an inner surface shows. An opening in a ring can expose the ring's inner wall.
        GrainFine variation applied to a surface or image, like film-like noise.
        Matched stillsStill frames compared under the same stated conditions: the same moment, size, and lighting.

      2. 3.3.1 Check silhouette, proportion, joins, and placement at full size and at thumbnail size. Then inspect texture, light, occlusion, and inner walls.

      3. 3.3.2 D-003 compared three changes: repairing the contour, moving grain behind the object, and adding a recessed aperture. John tentatively preferred the repaired contour, then questioned the form itself. The study produced stills and no rendered film.

      4. 3.3.3 A cleaner join can support the object without making it worth using. If the object adds little to the premise, sketch another visual relationship before polishing its surface. Source: form, material, and depth.

  4. 4 Show the action and its result before naming the benefit.

    1. 4.0 · Section glossary
      TermMeaning here
      RevealThe exposure of a result that changes what the viewer understands or expects. In SLASH_MY, the reveal is also the closing section, beats 112 to 128 (56 to 64 seconds), where the identity is completed.
      ComparisonShowing a difference that matters under stated conditions. Two versions of a chart side by side on the same scale make a comparison.
      DefectA failed relationship the viewer can recognize. Labels that have come loose from their anchors are a defect.
      RepairAn action that corrects a defect, as when labels snap back to their anchors.
      ResultThe state left to inspect after the action, for example the realigned layout held still.

    2. 4.1 Prepare a reveal with a specific question.

      1. 4.1.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the scene glossary and setup and payoff definitions.
        Sync landmarkThe moment within an event that picture and sound both align to: the result's visible arrival, its contact, or an audible attack. It is recorded with its units and shared by both sides.
        Continuity carrierAn object, relation, movement, or sound kept on purpose across a boundary. In SLASH_MY a written page becomes a graph node in the next scene.

      2. 4.1.1 Establish what is missing and why its appearance matters. Keep a recognizable carrier through the preparation, and hold the result after it arrives.

      3. 4.1.2 The AX DAY reconstruction’s particles share the title’s target geometry before the drop. The build prepares one particular result, and the solid letters and event facts then complete it.

      4. 4.1.3 For an explainer, a quiet uncovering or an early result can work better than a long build. Decide what the viewer needs to discover before choosing the mechanism. Source: meaningful reveal.

    3. 4.2 Hold the reference conditions steady in a comparison.

      1. 4.2.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the scene glossary.
        EndpointOne of the states being compared, for example the before and the after of a repair.
        Reference conditionA property held stable so that a difference can be interpreted. When comparing two mixes, matched loudness is one.
        ConfoundAnother changed property that could explain the result. If one mix plays louder than the other, loudness is a confound.

      2. 4.2.1 Name what the comparison is about: amount, position, correctness, speed, or appearance. Keep the labels, scale, units, and identity consistent with that question.

      3. 4.2.2 Use paired endpoints for exact comparison, an overlay for alignment, or sequential movement to trace one object, and keep enough context to read the difference.

      4. 4.2.3 When comparing sound treatments, match the listening level. Record which picture and sound properties changed, and keep uncertain responses in the record. Source: focused comparison.

    4. 4.3 Show what acts on the defect and what changes afterward.

      1. 4.3.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the scene glossary.
        MechanismThe process by which an action changes an object: what acts on what. In a repair scene, realigning, reconnecting, and updating a prop are different mechanisms.
        Payoff wordingThe text that names the benefit once the repair is done.

      2. 4.3.1 Keep enough order in the defective state that the viewer recognizes the system as it should work. A detached label or a stale connection may show the problem more clearly than jitter.

      3. 4.3.2 Show the action that corrects the defect, stop the instability that belongs to it, and keep the same objects in the completed result.

      4. 4.3.3 Hide the payoff wording in a review copy and ask whether the viewer can still identify the repaired relationship. Then restore the text and sound and check the full passage. Source: defect to repair.

  5. 5 Connect the scenes through retained identity, developing motifs, and changes in density.

    1. 5.0 · Section glossary
      TermMeaning here
      Continuity carrierAn object, relation, movement, or sound kept on purpose across a boundary. In SLASH_MY a written page becomes a graph node in the next scene.
      MotifA recognizable element that recurs and whose role can develop. SLASH_MY's strike and its hook are motifs.
      DensityHow fast information and events arrive and how many happen at once. Cut count is one part of it.
      RestTime that supports reading, anticipation, confidence, or recovery, for example a held frame where little new happens.

    2. 5.1 Decide what should survive the boundary.

      1. 5.1.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the sequence glossary.
        SeamThe boundary between two scenes or sections, where picture and sound pass from one to the next.
        HandoffThe transfer of an object, state, or sound across a seam, as when a page in one scene becomes a node in the next.
        Draw ownershipWhich part of the implementation draws an element at a given time. At a handoff, ownership passes from the outgoing scene's code to the incoming scene's.

      2. 5.1.1 Record the outgoing and incoming position, scale, orientation, crop, direction, and sound phrase. Keep only the relationships the next scene needs.

      3. 5.1.2 In the cited SLASH_MY page transition, the written page becomes a graph node. The source keeps its content and geometry, hands draw ownership to the next scene, and combines a pullback with a deliberate cut to rest.

      4. 5.1.3 Inspect frames before, on, and after the seam. Follow the page and listen across the cut, checking for duplication, replacement, or a camera reset. For a dense factual diagram, a fixed overview with an inset may work better. Sources: continuity carriers, page to larger context.

    3. 5.2 Give each return a changed role.

      1. 5.2.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the motif definition.
        InvariantThe property kept fixed so an element stays recognizable while other things about it change. A motif's shape can stay fixed while its color and size change.
        DevelopmentA change in role, arrangement, or consequence across a motif's appearances. SLASH_MY's hook is first heard as typing rhythm and later as the reveal's lead.

      2. 5.2.1 List the motif’s appearances. For each one, state what stays recognizable and what the viewer now knows.

      3. 5.2.2 SLASH_MY uses the caret first as John’s cursor and later as part of its identity. Its strike answers a different complaint each time, and its musical hook changes role across sections.

      4. 5.2.3 A recurring question, measurement, or viewpoint can make the same kind of connection. A short explanation may need only stable labels and clear state changes. Source: motif development.

    4. 5.3 Reduce activity where the viewer needs time.

      1. 5.3.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the density and rest definitions.
        ConcurrencyEvents that happen at the same time, like a title entrance and a cue.
        ForegroundThe visual or audible element that matters most in a passage, like the narration over a music bed.

      2. 5.3.1 Put words, movements, musical changes, and sound cues on one schedule. Look for collisions, and for long stretches that ask the viewer to do the same thing again and again.

      3. 5.3.2 SLASH_MY’s calmer writing passage changes palette, camera behavior, reading task, and groove at once, and its page becomes the next scene’s subject. Copying only the pale palette would keep one of those changes and lose the rest.

      4. 5.3.3 In the AX DAY reconstruction, the short gap before the drop serves anticipation and the longer title hold serves reading. Choose each interval for its task, and review the full rise, rest, and return. Source: density and rest.

  6. 6 Compose the score’s progression and give each sound a clear place in the mix.

    1. 6.0 · Section glossary
      TermMeaning here
      ScoreThe music made for the piece.
      BeatA unit of the score's pulse, the count you would tap along to. Case beat numbers use that case's tempo: at 120 BPM a beat is 500 ms, so beat 112 is 56 seconds in.
      MusicalityHow phrase, melody, harmony, groove, and development work together in the music.
      Sound productionBuilding cues, shaping their timbre, processing them, placing them in space, and setting the mix hierarchy.
      CueA sound placed at an action or moment, in the film-scoring sense. A whoosh as a page flies in is a cue.

    2. 6.1 Plan the musical relationships across scenes.

      1. 6.1.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the sound glossary.
        PhraseA musical passage with a beginning, a continuation, and an arrival, like a sentence in speech.
        MelodyAn organized line of pitches heard one after another, the part you could hum.
        HarmonyThe pitches sounding together and how they move from one chord to the next.
        GrooveThe pulse of the music and where the attacks of notes and drums sit against it.
        AugmentationLengthening a recognizable rhythmic figure, for example playing it so each note lasts twice as long.

      2. 6.1.1 Give the phrase an arrival and space after it, and line that arrival up with the visual result. When narration carries the explanation, spoken meaning can set the timing.

      3. 6.1.2 Develop recognizable material through rhythm, register, harmony, or arrangement. In the cited SLASH_MY score, the hook starts in the typing rhythm, changes with each section’s groove, slows in the writing passage, and comes back as the reveal’s lead.

      4. 6.1.3 Listen to bass attacks and kicks together. Ducking can remove a bass attack that lands on a kick. Audition a timing change within the full phrase before deciding it helps. Source: musical development.

    3. 6.2 Shape and separate the sounds that express the action.

      1. 6.2.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the sound glossary.
        EnvelopeThe change in a sound's level over its duration, through attack, body, and release: a plucked string starts sharply and fades slowly. Envelopes can also control a sound's pitch or timbre.
        TimbreA sound's audible character, the quality that tells a piano from a guitar playing the same note.
        MaskingOne sound making another harder to hear, as when a bass line covers the low part of a narrator's voice.
        DuckingLowering one sound while another plays, keyed to the second, such as dipping the chords after each kick or lowering the music while a narrator speaks.
        Send and returnA send copies part of a channel's signal to a shared effect such as reverb or delay; the return brings the processed (wet) signal back into the mix.

      2. 6.2.1 Align the important audible attack with the intended visual sync landmark. A cue can start on time and still reach its body after the contact.

      3. 6.2.2 Choose one foreground for each passage: speech, the musical hook, an action cue, or silence. Use level, spectral space, and routing to make that choice audible.

      4. 6.2.3 Decide which tails survive a cut. A deliberate silent gap may require muting the wet return as well as the dry source, and a continuity handoff may keep the tail.

      5. 6.2.4 Listen to the full mix, isolate one role to find the problem, then go back to the full mix. Compare versions at matched listening level. Source: sound production.

  7. 7 Build a workbench that reproduces the intended states and exposes delivery failures.

    1. 7.0 · Section glossary
      TermMeaning here
      RepresentationThe model used to draw the subject, such as a bitmap, vector, document layout, or 3D scene.
      Deterministic renderingRendering in which the same specified inputs and time produce the same intended frame. It supports repeatable capture and precise inspection; external assets and runtime behavior still matter.
      CaptureTo record frames from a running browser page, or collect rendered frames, for an output file.
      EncodingCompressing picture and sound with codecs into streams for a delivery file. Stage intro used H.264 video and AAC audio.
      DeliveryThe form of the piece the viewer actually receives, for example the encoded file a platform plays.

    2. 7.1 Choose representations and clocks for the required view.

      1. 7.1.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the workbench glossary.
        Requested timeThe time for which a frame is computed. A frame requested at 2.5 seconds shows the piece at 2.5 seconds, whatever the real clock says.
        SeedAn input that makes random variation repeatable: the same seed gives the same jitter on every render.
        BakeStore the calculated results of a simulation or other process so they can be used later without recalculating the whole history.
        Sync landmarkThe moment within an event that picture and sound both align to: the result's visible arrival, its contact, or an audible attack. It is recorded with its units and shared by both sides.

      2. 7.1.1 A flat image gives one view. A camera orbit that shows hidden surfaces needs more information, and text needs loaded fonts and measured bounds.

      3. 7.1.2 Make evaluation at a requested time reproducible. When frames are requested out of order, a stateful simulation needs fixed stepping, checkpoints, or a bake.

      4. 7.1.3 Record a shared event’s sync landmark and its units, then derive the visual and audio times from it. Trigger time can differ from visible arrival or audible attack. Reproducible frame state and byte-identical encoding are separate properties. Source: production workbench.

    3. 7.2 Write the boundary contract and inspect the actual file.

      1. 7.2.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the workbench glossary and handoff definitions.
        Coordinate spaceThe reference system that positions and transforms are measured in, like page pixels or a 3D scene's units.
        Overlap intervalThe time when the outgoing and incoming scenes are both present.
        Decoded fileThe delivery file read back into picture and sound for inspection.

      2. 7.2.1 Before composing the scenes, state the outgoing and incoming geometry, coordinate space, overlap, draw ownership, and the sound that carries across the boundary. For a small project, a short boundary table can be enough.

      3. 7.2.2 Inspect the first useful frame, the strongest treatment, the last required arrival, the boundaries, and the ending. Seek out of order as well as playing forward.

      4. 7.2.3 Decode and play the delivered file at delivery size. Check frame count, streams, color, opening behavior, and sound hierarchy. A preview can run on different clocks from capture, or have its assets ready at different times.

      5. 7.2.4 Adapt to the viewing medium. Vertical video needs stable captions and framing that suits the format. Pages the reader controls need to handle interruption and reversal, and newsletters and thumbnails need stills that work on their own. The upstream Awesome AI Motion collection’s medium map gives the general rule: choose the deliverable before carrying over a technique’s numbers, because time, navigation, available controls, screen size, and fallback behavior change what the same motion is for. Its seven deliverable routes pair each of recorded video, vertical feed, product intro, infographic, deck or site, newsletter, and thumbnail still with the adaptation that matters most for it. Source: production workbench.

  8. 8 Use evidence that can answer the review question and keep each finding within its conditions.

    1. 8.0 · Section glossary
      TermMeaning here
      Source inspectionCraft-shelf evidence label: code or documents implement or prescribe the behavior. This is evidence about the instructions, which is separate from watching or hearing what they produce.
      Rendered-frame inspectionCraft-shelf evidence label: a rendered frame or contact sheet shows geometry and appearance at the sampled moment, for example where a title sits in the frame.
      Continuous playbackCraft-shelf evidence label: watching the piece in motion. It supports judgments about duration, pacing, movement, and reading time.
      ListeningCraft-shelf evidence label: judging by ear how sounds relate, including musicality, timbre, masking, and the mixed result.
      Human feedbackCraft-shelf evidence label: an attributed judgment under the recorded viewing conditions, kept together with its tentative and negative responses.
      InterpretationEvidence label: a proposed explanation or design judgment, stated with the condition under which it helps and the observation that could challenge it.

    2. 8.1 Match the inspection to the failure.

      1. 8.1.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the evidence glossary.
        SymptomThe failure as observed, before its cause is known, for example text that shimmers during a pan.
        Candidate causeA possible explanation for a symptom, still to be tested.
        AblationRemoving or changing one limited part to compare against the original. The C2 one-kick ablation removed one extra kick and kept the rest of the score.

      2. 8.1.1 Describe the failure at a particular moment. Unreadable text may arrive late, move too long, crop badly, or compete with surface detail; choose a comparison that tells those possibilities apart.

      3. 8.1.2 Use stills for clipped text and broken joins, boundary frames for spatial resets, playback for pacing and reading time, and listening for groove, timbre, and masking. Read the source to find mechanisms worth testing.

      4. 8.1.3 After a local repair, replay the whole piece and check whether the change helps the premise, the scene’s task, and the sequence around it. Source: diagnose.

    3. 8.2 Preserve uncertain and negative findings.

      1. 8.2.0 · Subsection glossary
        TermMeaning here
        Shared termsUse the evidence glossary.
        SnapshotA recorded version of a source, named by its commit hash; SLASH_MY's audio snapshot is fd543f2a. A page's values hold for the snapshot it names.
        PreferenceA recorded judgment of one result over another, under recorded conditions.
        SidegradeA different result with no established improvement. Run D-005, which replaced the particle reveal with title exposures, was tentatively judged a sidegrade.
        Isolated causeA factor tested separately enough that an effect can be attributed to it.

      2. 8.2.1 The craft shelf records its inspected production sources at snapshot 32dec413e8f36d784cc7bdae8975d7eca3474093. It records John’s SLASH_MY preference from October 2, 2026, which supports SLASH_MY as an overall reference; the SLASH_MY case states what that verdict covers and why it names no isolated cause.

      3. 8.2.2 D-001 was judged acceptable but derivative. D-002 kept the production infrastructure and the encoded sound, and its new picture was judged weaker. These findings make the relationships between components worth studying.

      4. 8.2.3 D-007 changed when words appeared on exact cue frames, and John found little meaningful difference at playback speed. C2’s one-kick repair left both excerpts rated bad.

      5. 8.2.4 D-005 and D-006 were separate branches, and both were judged sidegrades.
