# Production lineage

Code-authored video uses operations that graphics, animation, sound, and editing systems have used for decades. The sources below show **how an operation was specified and used**, often under real production constraints. Each section pairs a documented mechanism with a decision you can make now. [Operations](../Operations.md) names the operations, and the linked theory pages explain the visual or perceptual reason to use them. This page traces the operations to external sources; [content lineage](Content%20lineage.md) records where each page of the package comes from.

## Decide what exists and when

### Give the timeline structure before animating properties

The W3C's 1998 [SMIL 1.0 recommendation](https://www.w3.org/TR/1998/REC-smil-19980615/) ([excerpt](excerpts/smil-time-groups.md)) describes independent media objects arranged in sequential and parallel time groups. It gives them begin times, durations, and a shared presentation clock. Unity's [2018.4 Timeline manual, published in 2019](https://docs.unity3d.com/2018.4/Documentation/Manual/TimelineOverview.html), shows the same production concern in a visual editor: tracks and clips form a reusable Timeline Asset, while a Timeline instance binds those tracks to particular scene objects. Unity's example reuses one victory sequence, including animation, music, and particles, for different characters.

**Current decision.** Write scenes and meaningful events as data before writing per-frame effects. One event such as `title_complete` or `impact` can schedule several visual and audio changes. Keep the reusable timing arrangement separate from the assets and objects assigned to it, so a sequence is easier to retime or reuse without duplicating its logic. These sources are where **schedule a meaningful event** and **align media to a common time** in [Operations](../Operations.md) come from. The design test is whether the order and spacing of events produce the [temporal rhythm](../theory/Temporal%20visual%20rhythm.md) you meant.

### Animate a property, not an effect name

The W3C's [SVG 1.1 animation specification of 14 January 2003](https://www.w3.org/TR/2003/REC-SVG11-20030114/animate.html) ([excerpt](excerpts/svg-animation-elements.md)) specifies values over time, `keyTimes`, discrete or interpolated changes, cubic Bézier `keySplines`, motion paths, transform animation, and additive animation. Its examples animate text visibility, position, color, rotation, and scale together. A later name such as “kinetic title” describes the combined result, but the controls underneath stay separate.

**Current decision.** For each moving element, specify the interval, property, start and end values, interpolation, and path separately. When frame seeking matters, make it possible to compute the property at any requested time. Then a title can move while its opacity holds, or reveal while its position stays fixed. This is the operation **evaluate a property at a chosen time** in [Operations](../Operations.md). Easing sets the rate of change. Whether the viewer has enough time to read the finished title is a separate question: check the [reading time](../theory/Moving-text%20legibility.md) once the title holds still, and the purpose of the motion in [Time and motion](../knowledge/Time%20and%20motion.md).

### Preserve object structure for controlled revision

The same 2003 SVG specification defines [groups, symbols, and reuse](https://www.w3.org/TR/2003/REC-SVG11-20030114/struct.html): a scene can retain individual objects and apply transforms or styles to a group. For larger 3D work, Pixar's [OpenUSD introduction](https://openusd.org/dev/intro.html) explains how a stage composes referenced assets, layers, overrides, and variants into a scene graph. Its account traces the system's production roots to earlier Pixar animation pipelines and dates the USD project to 2012. The two documents work at different scales, and both keep relationships that a flattened image loses.

**Current decision.** Keep a title, chart mark, camera, character, and background as named sources when later changes must reach each one on its own. Group related sources so a parent transform can move them together. When the piece needs variants, expose them at the source and do not duplicate rendered frames. Render to pixels only at output, or at a cache point you placed on purpose. These are the operations **represent a source**, **expose a decision as a parameter**, and **relate elements in space** in [Operations](../Operations.md). [Representation models](../knowledge/Representation%20models.md) explains which edits each model keeps available.

## Build the image from layers and fields

### Treat transparency as image data

Thomas Porter and Tom Duff's [“Compositing Digital Images,” July 1984](https://keithp.com/~keithp/porterduff/p253-porter.pdf) ([excerpt](excerpts/porter-duff-compositing.md)) describes combining separately rendered image elements using four-channel color and coverage. Their operators formalize relationships such as placing one layer *over* another. Pete Warden's later [account of Apple's Motion GPU pipeline](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-iii-high-quality-rendering/chapter-25-gpu-image-processing-apples-motion) reports alpha fringes and softening that appeared when images were filtered and moved through multiple passes.

**Current decision.** Define layer order, masks, opacity, and how alpha is handled at every handoff. When a scaled or blurred transparent asset develops a dark or bright edge, inspect how its RGB values and alpha are stored and filtered. A reveal mask is a coverage field that changes, and a dissolve changes coverage over time, so neither needs a separate primitive called a “transition.” Both apply **combine and reveal layers** and **evaluate a property at a chosen time** in [Operations](../Operations.md). Use [attention and hierarchy](../theory/Attention%20and%20hierarchy.md) to decide which layer should stay visually dominant.

### Use image-processing passes where they fit

Warden's [GPU Gems 2 chapter on Apple's Motion](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-iii-high-quality-rendering/chapter-25-gpu-image-processing-apples-motion), published in 2005 about work from 2003–04, describes a production video-effects pipeline. It identifies image-in/image-out processing as a good match for pixel shaders, uses off-screen buffers between passes, and discusses filtering, precision, motion blur, and CPU reference implementations. Its problems are still recognizable: a mathematically correct filter can look wrong after sampling, precision loss, or repeated rescaling.

**Current decision.** Use a shader or image filter when each output pixel can be calculated from the current image, nearby pixels, parameters, and time. Chain passes only when you need an intermediate result, and inspect the output resolution, alpha edges, and precision at each pass. For text and vector edges, decide whether to draw them after a low-resolution effect pass. This ties the operations **combine and reveal layers**, **sample a specified state**, and **compare output with the intended effect** in [Operations](../Operations.md) to [shaders and 3D](../knowledge/Shaders%20and%203D.md) and [verification](../knowledge/Verification.md).

### Generate coherent variation with a field

Ken Perlin's [GPU Gems chapter on improved noise](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems/part-i-natural-effects/chapter-5-implementing-improved-perlin-noise), published in 2004, explains a repeatable, smoothly varying function of position and its use in procedural texture. Simon Green's [2005 GPU implementation](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-iii-high-quality-rendering/chapter-26-implementing-improved-perlin-noise) shows the trade between calculating noise in a shader and sampling a stored 3D texture. In both accounts, noise is a signal you control, and the word is not another name for visual richness.

**Current decision.** Feed a stable field with position, and with time if needed. Map its output to one chosen property such as displacement, texture density, opacity, or color. Keep the field's scale and amplitude as parameters, so you can tune the variation to the job it does in the picture. If the same frame must render twice, the random choices feeding the field must be repeatable. These are the operations **repeat with controlled variation** and **evaluate a property at a chosen time** in [Operations](../Operations.md). Check whether the detail supports the [visual identity](../theory/Visual%20identity%20and%20style.md) you intend.

### Mix procedural motion with prepared media when the shot needs it

Hubert Nguyen's [“Fire in the ‘Vulcan’ Demo,” published in GPU Gems in 2004](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems/part-i-natural-effects/chapter-6-fire-vulcan-demo) documents an [NVIDIA demonstration released in August 2003](https://www.nvidia.com/en-gb/geforce/community/demos/). The team tested fully procedural particles and a screen-space distortion before using video-textured flame sprites, procedural smoke, emitter controls, and heat shimmer together. It varied each sprite's size, position, lifetime, start frame, and opacity to reduce visible repetition. It also rendered flame layers at reduced resolution to control fill cost.

**Current decision.** Separate the *appearance source* from its *placement and motion rules*. A sprite can hold organic detail that is hard to generate while code controls emitter position, timing, scale, overlap, and variation. A fully generated shape is useful when its structure must respond to the scene; a prepared image or clip is useful when its detail is already right for the view. Check the camera angle and look for repeated patterns, because either can give the method away. This combines the operations **represent a source**, **repeat with controlled variation**, **evaluate a property at a chosen time**, and **combine and reveal layers** in [Operations](../Operations.md). [Capability boundaries](../knowledge/Capability%20boundaries.md) gives the corresponding feasibility question for a requested shot.

### Encode a look with a color transform

Jeremy Selan of Sony Pictures Imageworks describes [production use of 3D lookup tables in GPU Gems 2, 2005](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-iii-high-quality-rendering/chapter-24-using-lookup-tables-accelerate-color). A 3D LUT maps an input color to an output color by sampling and interpolation. Selan describes using a compact LUT to preview complex color correction on high-resolution film imagery. Selan also states its boundary: a LUT cannot express an operation that depends on where a pixel is or on its neighbors, and a coarse or low-precision table can create visible errors.

**Current decision.** Use a color transform to keep a family of shots consistent in look when the change you want depends on each pixel's color. Choose its input color domain, its precision, and its place in the pipeline on purpose. Use masks, spatial filters, or separate layers for local effects such as a vignette or a grade on one subject. This extends the operations **bind a role to a visual language** and **combine and reveal layers** in [Operations](../Operations.md). [Delivery and color](../knowledge/Delivery%20and%20color.md) covers what must survive export for that look to display as intended.

## Make sound and final media

### Specify sound sources and a score separately

Max Mathews's 1961 Bell Labs paper, [“An Acoustic Compiler for Music and Psychological Stimuli” (original scan)](https://vtda.org/pubs/BSTJ/vol40-1961/articles/bstj40-3-677.pdf) ([journal issue, pp. 677–694](https://vtda.org/pubs/BSTJ/vol40-1961/bstj-vol40-issue03.html); [DOI record](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1961.tb03237.x)), describes programs that define instruments and a sequence of note instructions that plays them. The W3C's [Web Audio working draft of 8 December 2015](https://www.w3.org/TR/2015/WD-webaudio-20151208/) documents a modern signal graph: oscillators or recordings feed gain and processing nodes; `AudioParam` schedules envelopes; sources start at specified audio times; `OfflineAudioContext` renders a buffer without real-time playback.

**Current decision.** Write each sound event down first as a timed plan: onset, source, duration, envelope, and role in the mix. Generate or select the source separately, then render against one audio time coordinate. If video frames and audio samples come from different systems, convert cue times explicitly and inspect the combined file. These are the operations **shape one audible event**, **combine sound by role**, and **align media to a common time** in [Operations](../Operations.md). The perceptual question is whether the viewer hears and sees the sound and the visual event as one event; see [audiovisual synchronization](../knowledge/Audiovisual%20synchronization.md).

### Keep media assembly explicit

FFmpeg's [libavfilter 2.8 documentation](https://ffmpeg.org/doxygen/2.8/group__lavfi.html) documents graph-based frame editing in its 2015 release line. Its [filtergraph documentation](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html) specifies connected inputs, outputs, splits, and overlays, with current filters for text, audio fades, transforms, and more. The graph is a concrete way to combine generated frames, images, recorded footage, and sound after their individual sources have been authored.

**Current decision.** Treat assembly as a named graph: identify each input, its timing and transform, the point where visual and audio streams join, and the output format. A visual scene renderer can produce one input and leave every later crop, overlay, fade, or encode decision to the graph. Inspect the encoded result, because the live scene and the delivered file can differ in timing, color, sampling, and audio alignment. This connects the operations **combine and reveal layers**, **align media to a common time**, and **sample a specified state** in [Operations](../Operations.md) with [capture and encoding](../knowledge/Capture%20and%20encoding.md).

## A complete authored example

Grant Sanderson's [2017 *Essence of Calculus* lesson](https://www.3blue1brown.com/lessons/essence-of-calculus/) and its [published scene code](https://github.com/3b1b/videos/blob/master/_2017/eoc/chapter1.py) show an explanatory video built from programmatic objects, text, geometry, color roles, transforms, and timed reveals. The [Manim project](https://github.com/3b1b/manim) identifies itself as an engine for precise programmatic animation made for these videos. The code is evidence of the *construction*; the published video is the result to inspect for its explanation and pacing.

**Current decision.** Start a visual explanation by defining the concepts to compare or transform, then make those relationships editable objects. Reuse colors and object identities where they help the viewer follow a quantity or idea from one step to the next. The library can interpolate and render, but the maker still chooses which relationship to show, where attention goes, and how long the result stays readable. It is a worked example that combines **encode meaning in a visual property**, **relate elements in space**, **carry or break correspondence between states**, and **evaluate a property at a chosen time** in [Operations](../Operations.md). Review it through [grouping and continuity](../theory/Grouping%20and%20continuity.md) and [attention and hierarchy](../theory/Attention%20and%20hierarchy.md).
