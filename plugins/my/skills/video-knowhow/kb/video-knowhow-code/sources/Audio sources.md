# Audio source ledger

This ledger lists the verified sources behind the audio technique pages: [sound sources and voices](../knowledge/Sound%20sources%20and%20voices.md), [dynamics and spectral carving](../knowledge/Dynamics%20and%20spectral%20carving.md), [space and delay](../knowledge/Space%20and%20delay.md), [groove and feel](../knowledge/Groove%20and%20feel.md), [mastering and delivery](../knowledge/Mastering%20and%20delivery.md), [music under voice-over](../knowledge/Music%20under%20voice-over.md), and [sound and picture on one beat grid](../knowledge/Audiovisual%20synchronization.md#sound-and-picture-on-one-beat-grid). Each claim is worded to match what its source says. Quotes were checked against the fetched page on 2026-10-14 unless marked otherwise.

Each claim's source status is one of four: **supported** — the source says it; **partly** — reworded to fit the source; **practice** — common practice with no authoritative source found; **open** — not yet confirmed. A note after the status says when the source is secondary, a vendor's, a product review, or a single example. [Content lineage](Content%20lineage.md) records where the pages that cite this ledger come from.

## Feel and timing

| Claim | Status | Source | Quote |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roger Linn: 50% swing is straight, 66% a triplet; the useful range is about 50–70%. | supported | [Attack Magazine interview](https://www.attackmagazine.com/features/interview/roger-linn-swing-groove-magic-mpc-timing/) | "Between 50% and around 70% are lots of wonderful little settings" |
| At 96 BPM, laid-back snare strokes averaged 17.4 ms behind the click across 10 drummers (individual means −4 to +43 ms), about 29 ms later than their own on-beat strokes. | supported (computed from Table I) | Danielsen et al., JASA 138(4) 2015, [doi:10.1121/1.4930950](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.4930950) ([open PDF](https://pure-oai.bham.ac.uk/ws/files/44723836/Danielsen_et_al_2015.pdf)) | Table I: "Descriptive statistics of microtiming (TIM). Note: Timing in msec." |
| The timing JND for a displaced tone is about 6 ms for inter-onset intervals under ~240 ms and about 2.5% above that: ~6 ms for 16ths and 8ths at 120 BPM, ~12 ms for quarters. Musical training had no effect. | supported | Friberg & Sundberg, JASA 98(5) 1995, [doi:10.1121/1.413218](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.413218) | "absolute jnd… approximately constant at 6 ms for… intervals shorter than about 240 ms and the relative jnd constant at 2.5%" |
| Musicians' own timing deviations are long-range correlated (1/f-like), not white noise. Their standard deviation is about 12 ms for the most experienced players and about 21 ms on average. Use these figures as a measure of how players deviate. Do not copy them into a humanize control. No credible source gives a velocity-variation percentage. | supported | Hennig et al., PLoS ONE 6(10):e26457, 2011, [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0026457](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026457) | "the lowest values of the standard deviation of the time series were found for experienced musicians (σ≈12 ms, average standard deviation σ≈21 ms)" / "We found LRC in musical rhythms for all tasks and subjects" |
| Hennig et al. compared only two kinds of humanizing. They quantized a pop song, then added timing deviations with a standard deviation of 15 ms, once correlated (1/f) and once independent (white noise), each deviation shifting all tracks together. 39 choir members preferred the correlated version 64% of the time. The quantized original was not among the versions they heard, so the study does not show that humanized timing beats quantized timing. The authors have filed two patents related to the method. | supported | Hennig et al. 2011, Results and Methods; [2012 correction](https://doi.org/10.1371/annotation/f0a4c12c-ebef-4b55-9beb-2ca30749965e) | "The mean relative frequency with respect to preference for the 1/f version was 0.641 (SEM=0.040)" / "Drs Hennig, Fleischmann, Theis, and Geisel have filed two patents related to the algorithm employed in this study." |
| Professional swing and funk bass-and-drum performances had their microtiming scaled from 0% (quantized) up to exaggerated levels. Quantized and as-played versions rated equally high on groove, and ratings fell beyond the performed amount. Listeners: 79 experts and 81 non-experts. | supported | Senn, Kilchenmann, von Georgi & Bullerjahn, Front. Psychol. 7:1487 (2016), [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01487](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01487) | "both the fully quantized music and the music with the originally performed microtiming pattern were rated equally high on groove." |
| 93 music students rated a rock pattern with the bass drum or snare shifted ±15 or ±25 ms. The quantized version rated highest, and larger shifts rated lower. | supported (from the abstract) | Frühauf, Kopiez & Platz, Musicae Scientiae 17(2) 2013, [doi:10.1177/1029864913486793](https://doi.org/10.1177/1029864913486793) | "(a) The highest ratings of perceived drum pattern quality were given for the rhythmically accurate (quantized) version; (b) the increasing deviation in microtiming resulted in lower quality ratings" |
| In synthesized jazz, funk and samba patterns, systematic microtiming lowered groove, liking and naturalness ratings, except in a simple short-long jazz shuffle. | supported (from the abstract) | Davies, Madison, Silva & Gouyon, Music Perception 30(5) 2013, [doi:10.1525/mp.2013.30.5.497](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2013.30.5.497) | "systematic microtiming led to decreased groove ratings, as well as liking and naturalness, with the exception of the simple short-long shuffle Jazz pattern." |

Across the last three studies, quantized timing rated at least as high as microtiming, and exaggerated deviations rated lower. None found that microtiming raises groove over quantized timing. A swing ratio, as in Linn's swing setting, is a systematic pattern these studies did not test.

## Music under voice-over

These sources use different units: LU (a difference in BS.1770 integrated loudness), a PPM step (a peak-meter step), and WCAG's decibels (average RMS in its example, dB(A) sound pressure in its test). They do not convert exactly.

| Claim | Status | Source | Quote |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Torcoli et al. recommend a level difference (LD) of at least 10 LU for commentary over music and 15 LU for commentary over ambience, and more under location dialogue. Listeners accepted about ±3 LU around their preferred value. | supported | Torcoli et al. 2019 | "Esthetically pleasing upper limits can be considered 15 LU for CoM and 20 LU for CoA (i.e., the 75% quartile the non-expert participants' preferences)." |
